## What is a 2 metre acoustic barrier?

**A temporary acoustic barrier is a flexible, heavy panel with a sound-absorbing face, fixed to something that already stands up**: usually temporary site fencing, timber hoarding or scaffold. Panels join edge to edge to make a continuous noise screen as long as the site needs.

Two metres is the natural height for this kind of barrier, because that's the height of standard temporary fence panels. It's the most common form of construction noise screening in the UK, and on many sites it's all that's needed.

**Commonly used for:**

- Site perimeters next to houses, schools and offices
- Ground-level plant: generators, compressors, pumps, cutting stations, mixers
- Roadworks and utility works, where the barrier moves with the job
- Screening a work area within a larger site

## How much difference does it make?

A barrier works by blocking the direct path between the noise and the listener, so sound has to bend over the top. BS 5228-1, the UK code of practice for construction noise, gives a simple rule of thumb:

| Situation | Typical reduction |
|---|---|
| Barrier completely hides the plant from the listener | About 10 dB |
| Barrier just hides the top of the plant | About 5 dB |
| Plant still visible over or round the barrier | Little or none |

A 10 dB drop sounds roughly half as loud. That's a real improvement, but it depends entirely on the barrier breaking the line of sight.

**Rw (weighted sound reduction index) is a laboratory rating, in dB, of how much sound a panel stops passing straight through it.** It's useful for comparing products, but it's not the reduction you'll get on site, because on site sound also goes over the top and round the ends. That's why a well-fitted panel with a moderate Rw often beats a better panel that's badly installed.

**αw (weighted sound absorption coefficient) rates, from 0 to 1, how much sound the face soaks up rather than reflects.** A highly absorbent face stops noise bouncing around the site and out over the top. A barrier rated at 0.10 or below isn't classified for absorption at all and behaves much like solid hoarding.

## What good looks like

- **High enough:** hides the plant from the windows that matter, not just the ground floor
- **No gaps:** panels overlap or seal edge to edge, and the bottom sits on the ground
- **Close to the noise:** a barrier near the plant protects a much wider area than one on a distant boundary
- **Long enough:** runs past both ends of the noisy area, so sound can't go round
- **Absorbent face towards the works**
- **Tested figures:** an Rw to ISO 717-1 and an αw to ISO 11654 from a named test lab
- **Secure fixing:** panels that flap in the wind leave gaps and become a hazard

## When a 2 m barrier isn't enough

- **Upstairs windows and flats.** If the neighbours look down onto the works, a 2 m fence line doesn't block anything. You need a taller wall, or to deal with the noise at the machine.
- **Tall plant.** Piling hammers, crushers and tall rigs sit well above 2 m. See [acoustic barriers up to 8 metres high](/options/acoustic-barriers-up-to-8m) and [pile rig shrouds](/options/pile-rig-shrouds).
- **Impact noise right next to homes.** Breakers and hammers are often better tackled at source with a [rock breaker shroud](/options/rock-breaker-shrouds).
- **Where people need to see through it.** For sightlines, light or safety, look at [transparent acoustic barriers](/options/transparent-acoustic-barriers).

## Pros and cons

**Pros**

- Quick to fit and move on existing fencing, hoarding or scaffold
- Widely available to buy or hire
- Effective for ground-level neighbours and low plant when fitted properly
- An absorbent face also helps the people working on site

**Cons**

- Limited to about 2 m on standard fencing
- Little benefit for upper floors or tall plant
- Performance depends heavily on installation: gaps and loose panels undo it
- Headline "up to" figures on spec sheets can mislead; compare Rw and αw instead

## What to ask for

If you're specifying or hiring barriers, or checking a site next to your home, ask:

1. What is the panel's Rw, and which lab tested it to which standard?
2. What is its αw (ISO 11654)?
3. Does the barrier hide the plant from the nearest and highest affected windows?
4. How are joints and the base sealed?
5. Was the barrier chosen as part of a BS 5228-1 noise assessment?
