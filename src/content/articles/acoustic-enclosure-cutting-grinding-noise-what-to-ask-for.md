The whine of a disc cutter going through concrete slabs, again and again, a few metres from your window. On tight urban sites, cutting, grinding and core drilling often happen right by the boundary because there is nowhere else to do them. Under section 60 of the Control of Pollution Act 1974, your council can set the plant or machinery used, the hours of work and the noise levels allowed on a construction site.

If you're working from home, trying to settle a baby, or just want a quiet evening, that high-pitched noise is hard to ignore. And it can feel as if nobody on site is listening.

This guide explains one practical measure you can ask for: an acoustic enclosure around the noisiest task.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- What an acoustic enclosure is and how it differs from a noise barrier
- Which jobs on a building site are worth enclosing
- What an enclosure can and can't do, honestly
- The law that lets your council set methods, machinery and hours
- What to ask the contractor and Environmental Health

## What is an acoustic enclosure?

**An acoustic enclosure is a small tent or box made from sound-reducing panels that goes around one noisy task, so the noise is contained at source rather than screened at the site boundary.**

On UK sites the common type is a mobile enclosure on lockable castors. The operative does the cutting or grinding inside, with a strip curtain or door for access and for feeding materials in. Because it moves with the work, it can sit close to the task instead of being fixed in one place.

Two terms help when you read about these products:

- **Rw (weighted sound reduction index) is a lab measure of how much sound passes through a panel.** A higher number means less sound gets through the material itself.
- **Insertion loss is the drop in noise at a particular point once a barrier or enclosure is put in place.** This is what you actually notice at home, and it is almost always less than the lab figure.

You can read more in our guide to [what an Rw rating means](/articles/what-is-rw-rating-acoustic-barrier).

## Which noisy jobs are worth enclosing?

Not every noise on site suits an enclosure. They work best for a task that is intense, repeated and done in roughly the same spot. Hushtec's guidance for contractors lists these as the tasks to enclose first:

| Task | Why it suits an enclosure | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Slab, masonry and block cutting | Loud, high-pitched and repeated all day | Is the cutting done inside, with long pieces fed through the curtain? |
| Grinding and finishing | Constant, piercing noise | Is the curtain kept closed while grinding? |
| Core drilling | Long, steady noise in one spot | Are the castors locked and the panel joints closed? |
| Steel fabrication and fitting | Impact and cutting noise | Is the enclosure big enough for the lengths being worked? |
| Kerb, paver and stone cutting | Repeated cuts beside the material store | Is there a fixed cutting station inside an enclosure? |

If what bothers you is general site noise from lots of machines moving around, an enclosure alone is unlikely to fix it. That is where a boundary barrier comes in (see below).

## Can you ask the contractor to use one?

Yes. The straight answer is that you can't force a contractor yourself, but you can ask, and your council has real powers.

### Section 60: what your council can require

Under the **Control of Pollution Act 1974, section 60**, the council can serve a notice on whoever is carrying out construction works. The notice can:

- specify the plant or machinery which is, or is not, to be used
- specify the hours during which the works may be carried out
- specify the level of noise which may be emitted from the premises

When it does this, the council must have regard to the relevant code of practice (councils assess construction noise against BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014, the code of practice for noise on construction and open sites) and to the need for **best practicable means** to minimise noise. The contractor can appeal to a magistrates' court within 21 days.

**Best practicable means (BPM) means the steps that are reasonably practicable, having regard to local conditions, current technical knowledge and cost.** The legal definition in section 72 expressly includes the design and operation of plant and machinery and the design of "acoustic structures". An enclosure around a cutting station is exactly the kind of practical step BPM is about.

### Section 61: has the contractor agreed terms in advance?

Under **section 61**, a contractor can apply for prior consent before starting work. The application must set out the works, the methods and the steps proposed to minimise noise. The council must decide within 28 days and can attach conditions.

**A Section 61 consent is an agreement between the contractor and the council on how and when the work will be done, and how noise will be kept down.**

Sounds like it settles things, right? In practice it helps you most as a checklist. If a consent exists, ask Environmental Health what it says about cutting, working hours and screening, then compare that with what you see and hear.

### Statutory nuisance

Separately, under the **Environmental Protection Act 1990, section 79**, noise emitted from premises so as to be prejudicial to health or a nuisance is a statutory nuisance. The same section defines best practicable means using the same factors as above, so whether reasonable steps like an enclosure are being used matters here too. Our [statutory nuisance explainer](/learning-centre/what-is-a-statutory-nuisance-and-how-does-it-affect-you) covers this in more detail.

## What can an enclosure do, and what are its limits?

It depends mainly on three things: how well it is sealed, how close it is to the work, and what else on site is making noise.

**Pros**

- Contains noise at the source, so it helps every neighbour, including people in upper-floor flats
- Mobile, so it can follow the work around a tight site
- Small footprint; some models are narrow enough to sit inside a single-lane road closure
- Can be set up with the doorway facing away from the nearest homes

**Cons**

- Real-world reduction is lower than the lab rating, because of gaps, an open doorway, materials fed through the curtain and sound passing through the floor slab
- Only helps with the task inside it; other plant on site is unaffected
- Needs to be used properly every time: curtain closed, joints checked after each move
- The operative still needs hearing protection, dust control and, for grinding, a hot-work permit, which can make crews reluctant to use it if it is badly planned

## Enclosure, barrier or both?

![Long run of temporary acoustic barrier along a roadside site boundary, with an operative checking a joint](/images/barrier-roadside.webp)

**An acoustic barrier is a screen between the noise and you, usually fixed to the site hoarding, which interrupts the sound path for everything behind it.**

| Your situation | Likely better option |
|---|---|
| One loud task, repeated in one area (cutting, grinding, core drilling) | Enclosure around the task |
| General noise from lots of plant moving around the site | Boundary barrier |
| Tight site with homes on several sides | Both |
| You live in an upper-floor flat overlooking the site | Enclosure at source (a 2 m boundary barrier may not block your line of sight) |

Barriers have limits too. A lab Rw rating is not the reduction you get at your window, because sound diffracts over the top and around the ends of a barrier. If you can see the work over the top of the barrier, it is unlikely to be doing much for you. See [temporary acoustic barriers up to 2 m](/options/acoustic-barriers-up-to-2m) for more.

## What should you ask for?

Keep your requests specific and reasonable. That makes it easier for the site manager, and for Environmental Health, to act.

1. **Where is the cutting done?** Ask whether there is a fixed cutting station, and whether it can be moved further from homes or put inside an enclosure.
2. **Which way does the doorway face?** It should face away from the nearest occupied buildings.
3. **Is there a Section 61 consent?** Ask Environmental Health what it says about methods, hours and screening.
4. **Are boundary noise readings being taken?** Contractors are advised to log readings before and after putting measures in place. Ask whether these are shared.
5. **What are the working hours for noisy tasks?** Ask whether the loudest work can be grouped into set periods.

Keep a simple record while you wait for answers. Our guide to [recording construction noise evidence](/articles/how-to-record-construction-noise-evidence) explains what councils find useful.

## Before you act: checklist

- Identify the task that bothers you most: cutting, grinding, drilling, or general site noise
- Note when it happens and for how long
- Speak to the site manager first and ask where the cutting is done and whether it can be enclosed
- Ask Environmental Health whether a Section 61 consent is in place and what it requires
- Ask whether boundary noise readings are being taken
- If nothing changes, make a formal complaint with your records
