A repeated tapping or thudding that seems to come from inside your walls, starting in the afternoon and running into the evening, is one of the more unsettling kinds of construction noise. It is often pile driver noise, or the vibration from piling, carried into your home through the ground. Many councils recommend that noisy work stops by 6pm on weekdays, and City of York Council is one of them.

If you've spent evenings trying to work out where a noise is coming from, or lost a weekend to it, that's draining. You're not imagining it, and you don't have to just put up with it.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- What happened in a recent case in Osbaldwick, York
- Why piling noise can sound as if it's inside your walls
- What hours construction work usually keeps to, and who can change them
- Your rights under the Control of Pollution Act 1974 and the Environmental Protection Act 1990
- What a contractor can practically do, and when a barrier will and won't help

## What happened in Osbaldwick, York?

On 20 November 2026, YorkMix reported on residents in and around Osbaldwick who had been troubled by a repetitive tapping or popping noise. Some felt it as vibration as well as hearing it, and one thought it was coming from inside a wall.

Osbaldwick Parish Council first suggested the cause was piling on Outgang Lane, possibly carried along underground sewer pipes. A City of York Council environmental health officer then visited and found the source was works at Heritage House on Murton Way. The building was being underpinned to deal with subsidence, which needed some internal piling.

**Underpinning means strengthening a building's existing foundations, often by installing piles beneath or inside it.**

The officer said the contractor had timed the work so it would not affect neighbouring businesses and offices, which are busy during the day. After the officer's involvement, the contractor agreed that "there will be no further works on a weekend". It also agreed to try to start piling earlier, at 2pm, and finish by around 7pm. The works were expected to be complete within a week.

The case shows two things. First, tracking down the source of a noise can take a professional site visit. Second, a contractor's sensible-sounding reason for its timing can still leave residents carrying the cost, and a council can help rebalance that.

## Why does piling sound like it's inside my walls?

Piling doesn't just make airborne noise. It also sends vibration through the ground.

**Ground-borne or structure-borne noise is vibration that travels through soil, foundations, walls or pipes and is then heard as sound inside a building.**

That's why the noise can seem to come from a wall, a floor or a radiator rather than from outside. It also explains why the site can be hard to identify: the path the vibration takes isn't always the shortest one.

This matters for what you ask for. A fence or barrier blocks sound travelling through the air. It does nothing for vibration moving through the ground.

## What hours are construction sites allowed to work?

There's no single national rule. It depends on what your council recommends and on any conditions set for that particular site.

City of York Council recommends limiting noisy building work to:

| Day | City of York Council recommended hours |
|---|---|
| Monday to Friday | 8.00am to 6.00pm |
| Saturday | 9.00am to 1.00pm |
| Sundays and public holidays | No noisy work |

These hours are guidance, not law on their own. A council can make hours binding for a specific site using the powers below. Note that the York finish time agreed in this case, around 7pm, is still later than the council's recommended 6pm weekday finish. Agreements like this are a compromise for a short job, and it's reasonable to ask why particular hours were chosen.

## What are my rights?

### Control of Pollution Act 1974, section 60

Section 60 applies to building, demolition, road works and engineering construction. It lets your council serve a notice setting out how works must be carried out. The notice can specify:

- the plant or machinery that may or may not be used
- the hours during which the works may be carried out
- the level of noise that may be emitted

When setting requirements, the council must consider the relevant codes of practice, the need to use the best practicable means to minimise noise, and the need to protect people in the area.

**Best practicable means (BPM) is the legal test of whether a site is doing what is reasonably practicable to keep noise down, taking into account local conditions, current technical knowledge and cost.**

In England, the approved codes of practice are BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014 for noise and BS 5228-2:2009+A1:2014 for vibration.

### Control of Pollution Act 1974, section 61

**A Section 61 consent is prior approval a contractor can apply for, setting out its methods and the steps it will take to minimise noise.** The council must respond within 28 days. You can ask your council whether a site near you has one and what hours it allows.

### Environmental Protection Act 1990, statutory nuisance

Under section 79(1)(g), noise emitted from premises that is prejudicial to health or a nuisance is a statutory nuisance. Section 79(7) says "noise" includes vibration.

If the council is satisfied a noise nuisance exists, section 80 requires it either to serve an abatement notice or to take other steps to persuade the person responsible to deal with it. If those steps don't work, it must serve a notice.

## What should I do first?

1. **Log it.** Note the date, start and finish times, what you heard or felt, and which room. See [how to record construction noise evidence](/articles/how-to-record-construction-noise-evidence).
2. **Try to find the source.** Look for site boards, letters through the door, or ask your parish or ward councillor.
3. **Contact the contractor.** Ask what work is happening, the hours, how long it will last, and whether it has a Section 61 consent.
4. **Report it to Environmental Health.** Send your log and ask the council to investigate, including vibration.

## What can the contractor practically do?

It depends mainly on whether the noise is reaching you through the air, through the ground, or both.

| What you notice | Likely path | What helps most |
|---|---|---|
| Tapping or thudding indoors, felt in walls or floors | Ground and structure | Better timing, a lower-vibration piling method, shorter working periods |
| Loud bangs outdoors with the site in view | Air | Shrouds at the pile, perimeter acoustic barriers, timing |
| Both | Air and ground | A combination of the above |

For outdoor piling, the main options are a shroud around the pile or a perimeter wall. Indicative field figures for a no, half and full shroud on a pile driver were:

| Distance | No shroud | Half shroud | Full shroud |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 m | 100.0 dB | 93.2 dB | 92.8 dB |
| 10 m | 94.7 dB | 88.3 dB | 88.2 dB |
| 15 m | 89.9 dB | 86.7 dB | 82.5 dB |

These come from separate trials (a full shroud at Woodhall Spa, a half shroud at Ramsey and a published no-shroud baseline), so treat them as a guide only. Results vary with the rig, the pile, the ground and how measurements are taken.

A perimeter barrier needs to be tall enough to block the line of sight from your windows, with no gaps, as close to the source as practical, and with an absorptive face so sound isn't reflected back towards other homes.

Is a barrier the answer in a case like York? Probably not, because internal piling inside a building is mostly a ground-borne problem. That's why timing was the fix there. ## Before you act: checklist

- Have you logged dates, times, what you heard or felt, and where in your home?
- Do you know the source, or have you asked Environmental Health to help find it?
- Have you asked the contractor for its hours, its methods and how long the work will last?
- Have you asked whether there is a Section 61 consent, and what hours it allows?
- Is the problem airborne, ground-borne or both? That decides which fixes are worth asking for.
- Have you mentioned vibration as well as noise in your complaint?
