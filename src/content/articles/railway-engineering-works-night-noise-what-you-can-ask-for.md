If you live near a railway line, you may know the pattern: a letter (or no letter), then a night of floodlights, generators, reversing alarms and the grinding roar of track machines just metres from your bedroom window. Hushtec's guidance for rail contractors puts ballast tamping and rail grinding at **80–105 dB(A)**, and much of this work happens at night.

Losing sleep for nights on end is exhausting, especially if you have work, children or a newborn to think about. And it's easy to feel that because "it's the railway", nobody can do anything.

That isn't the case. Railway works are covered by the same construction noise law as any building site, and there is a lot you can reasonably ask for.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- Why railway work happens at night, and whether that's allowed
- Which laws and standards apply to railway engineering noise
- What night-time noise levels the British Standard treats as significant
- What practical measures you can ask the contractor for
- Who to contact, and in what order

## Why does railway work happen at night?

Network Rail says night work usually causes the least disruption to passengers, is safer for workers, and is necessary because it could not maintain its network within daytime hours alone. Most track work needs the line closed, which railway people call a **possession**: **a planned period when a section of line is handed over from train operations to engineering work.** Hushtec's rail guidance notes these windows can be as short as four hours.

So night working on the railway is often unavoidable. But "unavoidable" isn't the same as "anything goes". Network Rail's own best practicable means guidance (NR/GN/ESD25) says noisy work audible at the boundary should be done in normal daytime hours where possible, and that **night working should be considered a last resort**.

## Does construction noise law apply to the railway?

Yes. The Control of Pollution Act 1974 defines works of engineering construction to include "the construction, structural alteration, maintenance or repair of any railway line or siding" (section 73). That brings railway works within the Act's construction noise powers.

**Section 60 notice.** Your council can serve a notice on whoever is carrying out the works, specifying the plant or machinery that may or may not be used, the hours of work, and the noise levels allowed. Breaching a notice without reasonable excuse is an offence.

**Section 61 prior consent.** **A Section 61 consent is advance approval a contractor applies for, setting out the works, the methods and the steps it will take to minimise noise.** The council has 28 days to decide and can attach conditions. Network Rail's BPM guidance lists obtaining Section 61 consent as a core step, and says plant on site should be checked against the plant list in the application.

**Best practicable means (BPM).** **BPM is the legal test of whether a contractor is doing what is reasonably practicable to reduce noise, having regard to local conditions and circumstances.** It's defined in section 79(9) of the Environmental Protection Act 1990, and councils must consider it when setting section 60 requirements.

**Statutory nuisance.** Under section 79(1)(g) of the Environmental Protection Act 1990, noise emitted from premises that is prejudicial to health or a nuisance can be a statutory nuisance. Under section 82, you can complain directly to a magistrates' court after giving at least three days' written notice. A Section 61 consent must state that it does not, by itself, give a defence in those proceedings.

Very large schemes, such as HS2, also have project-specific thresholds set in their Environmental Statements, and HS2 Ltd publishes regular noise and vibration monitoring reports. Ask the project team which thresholds apply near you.

## What night-time noise level does BS 5228 treat as significant?

**BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014 is the British Standard code of practice for noise control on construction and open sites.** Its informative Annex E gives example thresholds for when site noise at homes may have a significant effect. The thresholds depend on how noisy your area normally is: Category A for quieter areas, B and C for progressively noisier ones.

**dB LAeq is the average sound level over a period, A-weighted to match how the human ear hears.**

| Time period | Category A | Category B | Category C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Night (23:00–07:00) | 45 dB | 50 dB | 55 dB |
| Evenings and weekends | 55 dB | 60 dB | 65 dB |
| Weekday daytime (07:00–19:00) and Saturday (07:00–13:00) | 65 dB | 70 dB | 75 dB |

*Source: BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014, Table E.1. "Evenings and weekends" means 19:00–23:00 weekdays, 13:00–23:00 Saturdays and 07:00–23:00 Sundays. These are examples, not legal limits.*

The standard also gives example criteria for offering **noise insulation** (for example secondary glazing) and, at higher levels, **temporary rehousing**. Both apply only when high noise lasts for 10 or more days of working in any 15 consecutive days, or more than 40 days in any six consecutive months. In the example table, the night-time insulation trigger is 55 dB LAeq (or 5 dB above the normal pre-works level, if that is higher); rehousing triggers are 10 dB higher. Again, these are not automatic entitlements, but they're a fair basis for a question if works near you will run for weeks.

## What can the contractor actually do about it?

It depends mainly on three things: what the noisy activity is, how close it is to you, and whether the worksite stays put or moves along the line.

### Static compounds and generators

Compounds, welfare units and lighting towers often run all night. Network Rail's guidance prefers mains power over generators. Where generators are needed overnight, it says they should be supersilenced and screened or enclosed, and static plant can be sited in excavated areas below ground level.

### Reversing alarms, idling and lighting

These are often the noises that wake people, rather than the main work. Network Rail's guidance says alarms should be set to the lowest safe output, with broadband or self-adjusting alarms where practicable, and that equipment and vehicles should be shut down when not in use.

### Barriers along the works

**A temporary acoustic barrier is a movable panel wall that blocks the direct path of sound between the work and your home.** Network Rail's guidance says barriers should be imperforate, of suitable mass, have an absorptive lining and extend at least 1 m above the highest sight line.

Commonly used for:

- Static compounds and generator areas
- Breaking-out and cutting work
- Longer jobs in one location, such as bridge or station works

**Pros**

- Cuts the direct sound path, especially for ground-floor rooms
- An absorptive face reduces sound bouncing back across the line to homes opposite
- Can be moved as the work moves

**Cons**

- Needs space beside the track, which isn't always available
- Less effective for upstairs windows that can see over the top
- Harder to use on fast-moving works such as tamping or grinding, which can travel a long way in a night

Two terms help you judge what's offered. **Rw (weighted sound reduction index) is a lab measure of how much sound passes through a barrier panel.** **Insertion loss is the drop in noise you actually get at a particular point once the barrier is in place.** On site, sound goes over the top and round the ends of a barrier, so insertion loss at your window is lower than the lab Rw figure.

## What questions should you ask?

| Ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is there a Section 61 consent for these works? | It shows the agreed hours, plant and noise measures |
| Why does this work need to be at night? | Night work should be a last resort under Network Rail's own guidance |
| Can generators be swapped for mains power, enclosed or moved? | Overnight generators are a common, fixable cause of complaints |
| Are reversing alarms broadband or set to minimum safe output? | Tonal beeping carries far and wakes people |
| Will barriers be used near homes, and how high? | They should be at least 1 m above the highest sight line |
| Is noise being monitored, and will results be shared? | You can compare levels with consent limits and BS 5228 thresholds |
| Is there a noise insulation policy for long works? | Long, loud works may meet the BS 5228 example criteria |

## Before you act: checklist

- Note the dates, start and finish times and the type of noise (tamping, grinding, generators, alarms, shouting)
- Check whether you received notice of the works and what it said
- Contact the project or the Network Rail Helpline first, with specific times
- Ask Environmental Health whether a Section 61 consent or section 60 notice applies
- Ask about generators, alarms, barriers and monitoring using the questions above
- If works will run for weeks, ask whether noise insulation has been considered
- If nothing changes, take your log to Environmental Health
