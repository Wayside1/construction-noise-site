A data centre build is not like a housing site. The noise is often less about short, sharp bangs and more about plant that keeps going: site generators, pumps, compressors and, towards the end of the job, the testing of the building's own standby generators. techUK puts typical generator noise at around 70–90 dB at source.

If you live next door, a drone that runs into the evening is hard to tune out, especially if you work from home or have children trying to sleep. If you run the site, you are fielding the complaints, and you may have 2 m acoustic panels on the fence already and still be getting calls from Environmental Health.

This guide explains how high a generator noise barrier needs to be on a data centre site, and what else to look at before you reach for a taller wall.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- Why steady plant noise needs a different approach from short bursts of noise
- How high a screen needs to be around a generator, with a simple rule of thumb
- How to judge whether a barrier is any good (Rw, αw, gaps and position)
- What the options are for plant noise screening at 4 m and above
- What to ask the contractor or the council for

## What makes data centre construction noise different?

Three things, mainly.

**It runs for long periods.** Temporary generators often power a site until the permanent grid connection is ready. Later, the data centre's own standby generators are installed and tested before the building goes live. Once a data centre is operating, testing is usually tightly controlled. One Environment Agency noise management plan for a data centre in Park Royal, west London, limits testing to half an hour a month, between 08:00 and 18:00. During construction, the controls come from the planning conditions and the council instead, so it is worth asking what they are.

**It is low-pitched.** Engine and fan noise has a lot of low-frequency content. Low-frequency sound bends over barriers more easily than higher-pitched noise, so a screen does less against a deep hum than its headline figures suggest.

**The plant is big.** Containerised generator sets, transformers and cooling plant are often taller than standard fence panels. That is where screening often falls short.

## How high does a generator noise barrier need to be?

**Hushtec's working rule of thumb is that an acoustic screen should be about 1.5 m higher than the top of the noise source.** So a 2.5 m generator needs a 4 m wall.

| Height of the top of the noise source | Screen height to aim for |
|---|---|
| 1.0 m (small pump or compressor) | 2.5 m |
| 2.5 m (for example, a large site generator) | 4 m |
| 3.5 m | 5 m |
| 4.5 m | 6 m |
| 6.5 m | 8 m |

Treat this as a first estimate, not a substitute for a noise assessment. It assumes listeners at ground level. If windows overlook the generator from first or second floors, the wall needs to be taller, or moved closer to the plant, so it still blocks the line of sight from the highest affected window.

The reasoning, and where the rule stops working, is covered in more detail in [How high does an acoustic wall need to be to screen generator and plant noise?](/articles/how-high-acoustic-wall-generator-plant-noise)

### Why does the extra height matter?

**A noise barrier works by blocking the straight line between the noise source and the listener.** Sound has to bend over the top and loses energy doing so.

BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014 is the code of practice used to predict and control construction noise in the UK. Under the prediction method commonly used with it, a barrier is credited with about 5 dB when the top of the plant is just visible over it and about 10 dB when the plant is completely hidden. If the plant is in clear view, the barrier gets no credit at all. A 10 dB drop sounds roughly half as loud, so the extra height is what turns a modest gain into a useful one.

Sounds simple enough, right? In practice, the generator also needs air. A screen that sits tight around a set can starve it of cooling air or trap hot exhaust. Any close-in screen or enclosure should be agreed with the generator supplier.

## What makes a good acoustic barrier?

Whether you are a resident looking over the fence or a site manager choosing a product, check these points:

- **Height above the line of sight.** You should not be able to see the top of the plant from the nearest windows.
- **No gaps.** Joints should overlap or be sealed, and the barrier should meet the ground. Noise leaks through small gaps.
- **Long enough.** The screen should run well past both ends of the plant, or wrap round it, so noise does not come round the sides.
- **Close to the source.** A barrier works best close to the plant or close to the homes, not halfway between.
- **Absorptive face towards the works.** A hard face reflects noise back across the site and towards other neighbours.
- **A published Rw and αw**, with the test standard named.

### What does Rw mean?

**Rw (weighted sound reduction index) is a single-number laboratory rating of how much sound a material stops passing through it, calculated to ISO 717-1 from measurements made to ISO 10140.** A barrier rated Rw 26 dB cuts sound passing straight through the panel by roughly 26 dB in the lab.

On site you will not see the full figure. Sound goes over the top and round the ends of the barrier, so the reduction you actually hear (the **insertion loss**, the difference in level with and without the barrier) depends mainly on height, length and position. Once the material blocks more than the sound going over the top, adding Rw helps less than adding height.

### What does αw mean?

**αw (weighted sound absorption coefficient) is a rating from 0 to 1, set under ISO 11654, of how much sound a surface soaks up rather than reflects.** An αw of 1.0 is Class A, the highest. An αw of 0.10 or below is not classified under ISO 11654 and is effectively reflective, like plywood hoarding.

## What are the options for plant noise screening on a data centre site?

| Option | Typical height | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acoustically enclosed generator set | Built in | Long-running generators | Must be specified at hire; still needs siting with care |
| Fence-mounted acoustic panels | About 2 m | Low plant, pumps, hand tools | Too low for large generators |
| Scaffold-mounted barrier | Flexible | Tall screens where scaffold is on site | Wind loading on a solid face needs design; slower to move |
| Stacked containers, lined | In container-height steps | Heavy, long-term screens | Cranage, footprint, reflective unless lined |
| Freestanding acoustic wall system | 4 m to 8 m | Generator compounds, plant yards | Needs level ground and access; higher cost than fence panels |

Before any of these, ask whether the noise can be reduced at source: a quieter or smaller set matched to the real load, a battery or hybrid unit that lets the engine stop overnight, an earlier mains connection, or simply moving the plant behind a site cabin or away from homes.

### Who is a tall wall NOT right for?

- **Small, low plant.** A 1 m pump does not need a 4 m wall; good fence panels will do.
- **Homes that look down on the plant from above.** No practical wall will hide it. An enclosure or a roofed screen is the better option.
- **Very low-frequency hum.** A quieter generator or a battery unit will usually do more than extra height.
- **Short jobs.** For a few days of work, moving the plant or changing hours may be quicker.

## What can you ask the contractor or the council for?

Under Section 60 of the Control of Pollution Act 1974, the council can serve a notice specifying the plant or machinery that may be used, the hours of work and the noise levels allowed. Contractors can apply in advance for prior consent under Section 61, setting out the works, the methods and the steps they will take to minimise noise.

Useful questions to ask:

1. How tall is the generator or plant, and how tall is the screen around it?
2. Can the plant be seen over the screen from the nearest homes, including upstairs windows?
3. Does the BS 5228 noise assessment assume a barrier, and what reduction does it allow for it?
4. What hours will generators run, and when will the standby generators be tested?
5. Can the plant be moved, swapped for a quieter set or switched off overnight?

## Before you choose or complain: checklist

- Measure or ask for the height of the top of the noise source, then add about 1.5 m as a first estimate.
- Check the view from the highest window that overlooks the plant.
- Look for gaps at joints and at the base, and check the screen extends past the ends of the plant.
- Ask for the Rw and αw, with the test standards named.
- Keep a record of when the noise happens and how long it lasts.
- Ask whether the noise can be cut at source before relying on height.
