When a site needs a tall noise wall in a hurry, three answers come up again and again. A local farmer suggests straw bales. The plant hire desk suggests stacking shipping containers. And a barrier supplier suggests a purpose-built acoustic wall.

If you live next door, you may simply be wondering why a wall of containers or bales has appeared at the end of your garden, and whether it will make any difference. If you run the site, you want to know which option will keep the council happy without blowing the budget.

All three can work. They work in different places, for different lengths of time, and they fail in different ways. This guide sets them side by side, honestly.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- What makes any noise wall work, whatever it is made of
- Where straw bales are a sensible choice, and where they are not
- What stacking containers really involves
- How a freestanding acoustic wall compares
- How to choose, with a side-by-side table and a checklist

## What makes any temporary noise wall work?

**A noise wall works by blocking the straight line between the noise source and the listener.** Sound that has to bend over the top loses energy; sound with a clear path does not. Under the BS 5228-1 prediction method, a barrier is credited with about 5 dB when the top of the plant is just visible over it and about 10 dB when it is completely hidden.

So before comparing materials, three questions decide most of the result:

1. **Is it high enough?** As a first estimate, Hushtec's rule of thumb is that the wall should be about 1.5 m higher than the top of the noise source. More on this in [How high does an acoustic wall need to be?](/articles/how-high-acoustic-wall-generator-plant-noise)
2. **Is it close enough to the noise?** A wall near the machine hides it from more windows than the same wall on the boundary.
3. **Is it continuous?** Gaps at joints, at the base and round the ends let sound straight through.

The material then decides two further things: how much sound passes *through* the wall (mass and airtightness), and how much bounces *off* it (absorption).

## Straw bales: cheap, absorptive and short-lived

People usually say "hay bales", but straw is what tends to get used. Hay is animal feed and worth more, and damp hay can heat up as it ferments.

**Why it can work.** Baled straw is dense. Research presented to the Institute of Acoustics notes that construction-grade bales typically weigh 80–180 kg per cubic metre. Straw is also fibrous, so it absorbs sound rather than reflecting it. Festivals have used straw walls with real success: at one Cumbrian festival, an organiser built walls of about 6 m wide by 3 m high from 50 densely packed bales around the loudest sound systems. He reported that the walls let the systems run 10–15 dBA louder than the year before while staying within limits at nearby homes.

**Where it falls down on a construction site:**

- **Height.** Small bales stack by hand to about head height; larger walls need big square bales and a telehandler. Around 3 m is a practical limit for a stable bale wall.
- **Gaps.** Bales are not uniform. Every joint is a potential leak unless the wall is staggered and packed tight.
- **Fire.** Bales themselves are hard to ignite, but the same research points out that loose straw on the ground is highly flammable. Hot works, discarded cigarettes and vandalism are real risks on a building site.
- **Weather and durability.** Wet bales get heavier, sag and rot. Over a long job you may end up replacing them, and they attract vermin.
- **Footprint.** A bale wall is wide, which uses up working space.

**Best for:** short events and short, low-level jobs on rural sites with plenty of room, where low cost and a soft, absorptive face matter more than height or lifespan.

## Shipping containers: tall and solid, but heavy going

**Why it can work.** A container is a sealed steel box, heavy enough to stop a lot of sound passing through it. It is also tall straight away: a standard container is about 2.6 m high and a high-cube about 2.9 m, so two high gives roughly 5.2–5.8 m and three high roughly 7.8–8.7 m.

Containers have screened some big projects. During the demolition of Tottenham Hotspur's old stand in London, a supplier stacked 50 forty-foot containers three high to form a wall around 170 m long and 7.6 m high between the works and nearby flats. In the Netherlands, 52 storage containers were stacked four storeys high as a site noise wall, and doubled as storage space.

**What it really involves:**

- **Cranage and access.** Every container arrives by lorry and needs a crane or HIAB to place it. On the Tottenham job, the client also had to provide a cherry picker and an appointed person to unhook containers at height.
- **Ground and stability.** The base course sat on railway sleepers, and each bottom container was loaded with about 12 tonnes of ballast, with heavy-duty interlocks between the levels. Stacked containers need firm, level ground and a proper lifting and stability plan.
- **Footprint.** A container is about 2.4 m wide, so the wall takes a 2.4 m strip along its whole length. On a tight urban site that is a lot of lost space.
- **Reflections.** Bare corrugated steel reflects sound. A container wall can protect homes on one side while bouncing noise back across the site or onto homes opposite. That is why many container walls end up with acoustic blankets or panels hung on them anyway.
- **Moving it.** Re-positioning a container wall as the work moves means bringing the crane back.

**The honest point in their favour:** a container wall can also be your site storage or welfare space. If you need the boxes anyway, using them as a wall makes good sense, especially with absorptive panels on the side facing the works.

**Best for:** long jobs on sites with space, crane access and hardstanding, where the wall stays in one place and the containers earn their keep as storage too.

## RapidWall: a purpose-built freestanding acoustic wall

![Two-tier freestanding acoustic wall with an angled return, set on concrete ballast blocks in woodland](/images/rapidwall-bushland.webp)

**How it works.** A freestanding acoustic wall is a steel frame held down by ballast, with acoustic panels hung on it. Hushtec's version, RapidWall, needs no ground anchors, footings or excavation. Standard heights are 2 m, 4 m, 6 m and 8 m, with other heights on request. It is wind certified to 31 m/s, its panels have a sound insulation rating of 32 dB, and it can be laid out as a U-shape, L-shape, perimeter or corridor.

The photo above shows what that means in practice: ballast blocks placed on bare ground, a galvanised frame, two tiers of panels hung from a scissor lift, and an angled return to stop sound escaping round the end. It stands among mature trees without any digging near their roots.

**Strengths:**

- **Absorptive face.** The panels soak up sound instead of reflecting it, so the noise is not simply redirected to other neighbours.
- **No digging and modest ground loads.** This matters on sensitive sites, near tree roots, over services or on ground you are not allowed to disturb.
- **Slim footprint.** The wall takes far less working space than a row of containers or bales.
- **Movable.** Panels and frames can be relocated as the work progresses, without a crane.
- **Height to suit.** Set it to the height the sight lines need, rather than in fixed container steps.

**Limitations:**

- **Cost.** It costs more than a few loads of straw.
- **Single purpose.** Unlike a container, it will not double as storage.
- **Wind and ground checks.** Like any tall temporary structure, it needs reasonably level ground, delivery access and a wind check for the site.

It is one option alongside containers, scaffold-mounted barriers and bales. The right choice depends on the site. See [acoustic barriers up to 8 m](/options/acoustic-barriers-up-to-8m) for more on tall walls.

## What does each option really cost?

The hire price is only part of it. Before comparing quotes, add up:

- **Delivery and collection.** Containers and wall systems both need lorries; bales usually come from the nearest farm.
- **Lifting.** Containers need a crane or HIAB for every box, both ways. This often costs more than the container hire itself.
- **Labour and time on site.** How many people, for how many days, and what access equipment?
- **Ground preparation.** Hardstanding or sleepers for containers; little or none for bales or a ballasted wall.
- **Lining.** Acoustic blankets to stop a container wall reflecting noise.
- **Moves.** How many times will the wall need re-positioning during the job?
- **Replacement.** Bales that rot or burn have to be replaced.

## Before you choose or complain: checklist

- Measure the top of the noise source and add 1.5 m as a first estimate of wall height
- Check the sight line from the highest affected window
- Put the wall as close to the noise as the work allows
- Look for gaps: between bales, between containers, at the base and round the ends
- Ask whether the face towards the works is absorptive or bare steel
- Check who has looked at wind loading and ground bearing for anything over about 2 m
- Count the real cost: transport, lifting, labour, lining and moves, not just hire
