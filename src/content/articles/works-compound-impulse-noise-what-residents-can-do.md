A steady hum is annoying. A sudden crash every few minutes is worse, because you can't tune it out. If a works compound near your home is loading heavy materials into trucks, you may be living with exactly that: sharp, unpredictable bangs through the working day, from 7am on some sites.

If you're flinching at every crash, struggling to work from home, or worried about what the vibration is doing to your walls, that's a hard way to live. You're not being fussy. The good news is that impulse noise is something a site can measure, and something it can usually reduce.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- What happened in a recent case on the Blackpool seafront
- Why average noise readings can hide the problem, and what to ask for instead
- Your rights under the Control of Pollution Act 1974 and the Environmental Protection Act 1990
- What a contractor can practically do about banging from materials handling
- A checklist to use before you make your next complaint

## What happened in Blackpool?

On 9 November 2026, Central Radio North West reported on residents of two apartment blocks on Queens Promenade, Blackpool. Across the road is a works compound for the £31 million Anchorsholme Coastal Protection Scheme. Blackpool Council commissioned the scheme, Balfour Beatty is the contractor and the Environment Agency funds it.

The residents have written a formal complaint to the council. Their main issue isn't steady background noise. It is sudden impulse noise from heavy machinery handling rock armour of up to 17 tonnes, loaded into unclad dumper trucks. They have asked for the maximum noise levels recorded at the boundary monitors to be published, for measures to reduce the impulse noise, and for the council to check whether the noise management plan is fit for purpose.

Some residents also link cracks in their walls to the work. An independent structural assessment arranged by the council concluded the works are not adversely affecting nearby properties, a finding the residents dispute.

Both sides have a case. The residents' spokesperson said "the coastal protection scheme is vital and needs to be carried out". The council says it meets and updates residents, has a public liaison officer, and that the contractor monitors sound during working hours. Its scheme page says vibration is monitored continuously, with work pausing if limits are exceeded. The revetment work is due to finish in summer 2027, with rock groynes following from spring 2027 to early 2028.

That's a long time to live next to a problem, so it's worth getting the approach right.

## What is impulse noise, and why do averages hide it?

**Impulse noise is a short, sudden sound, such as a bang or crash, rather than a steady one.** Rock dropped onto a bare steel truck body is a classic example.

Three terms matter here:

- **dB(A) is a decibel reading weighted to match how the human ear hears.**
- **LAeq is the steady level that carries the same total sound energy as the fluctuating noise over a measurement period.** In plain terms, an average.
- **LAmax is the highest level recorded during the measurement period.** The loudest single moment.

Here's the catch. A few loud bangs in an otherwise quieter hour may not lift the hourly average much. So a site can report a modest LAeq while you're jumping out of your chair. That is why asking for maximum levels, as the Blackpool residents did, is a sensible move.

## What are my rights about construction noise?

**Section 60 of the Control of Pollution Act 1974** lets your council serve a notice on anyone carrying out construction works. That covers building, demolition, roads and engineering construction work. The notice can specify which plant may or may not be used, set working hours and set noise levels. Breaking it without reasonable excuse is an offence.

**Section 61 is prior consent: the contractor agrees its methods, hours and noise controls with the council in advance.** The council must decide within 28 days. Working to a consent is a defence to a section 60 prosecution, but the consent must state that it is not, by itself, a defence to proceedings brought under section 82 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990.

**Statutory nuisance is noise that is "prejudicial to health or a nuisance"**, under section 79(1)(g) of the Environmental Protection Act 1990. Councils must take reasonably practicable steps to investigate complaints from people living in their area. If they agree a statutory nuisance exists, they must serve an abatement notice, although for noise they may first try other steps to get it stopped.

**Best practicable means (BPM) means the reasonably practicable steps a contractor should take to minimise noise**, considering local conditions, technical knowledge and cost. Where the noise comes from industrial, trade or business premises, the person responsible can defend a prosecution for breaching an abatement notice by showing BPM was used. In England, the approved code of practice is **BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014** for noise, with BS 5228-2 for vibration.

### What hours are typical?

It depends on what's agreed for the site. North Yorkshire Council, for example, recommends noisy work 8am to 6pm on weekdays, 8am to 1pm on Saturdays and none on Sundays or bank holidays. Some schemes publish their own hours. The Anchorsholme scheme's published hours are 7am to 7pm, Monday to Friday. Ask what has been agreed for your site, and log anything outside it.

## Who should I contact, and what should I ask for?

Start with the contractor, then go to the council if that doesn't work. North Yorkshire Council gives the same advice.

| Your concern | Who to contact first | What to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden bangs from loading or handling | Site manager or public liaison officer | Monitoring results (LAeq and LAmax) for the times you logged; what's being done about impact noise |
| Noise outside agreed hours | Site manager, then Environmental Health | The agreed hours; whether a section 60 notice or section 61 consent is in place |
| Possible damage from vibration | Contractor's vibration contact | Vibration monitoring results; a structural survey of your property |
| No response, or you disagree with the answer | Environmental Health, copying your ward councillor | A formal investigation under the Environmental Protection Act 1990 |

When the council is also the scheme's client, as in Blackpool, Environmental Health still has its own duty to investigate. Put everything in writing and keep copies.

## What can a contractor do about banging from materials handling?

Quite a lot, and the most effective fixes deal with the impact itself. Here are the main options.

### Reduce the drop

**Commonly used for:** loading rock, rubble or scrap into trucks and skips.

**Pros:** costs little; tackles the bang at source. HSE's general advice is to reduce the height and speed of falling objects.

**Cons:** can slow loading; depends on operator discipline.

### Line the truck bodies

**Commonly used for:** dumper and dump truck bodies, hoppers and chutes.

**Pros:** HSE reports a quarry cut noise by 14 dB after lining its 20-tonne dump truck bodies with abrasion-resistant rubber, where first loads onto bare metal had produced 96 dB(A).

**Cons:** that was one operation, with figures supplied by the lining's maker, so results will vary. It may not suit every vehicle.

### Move or schedule the activity

**Commonly used for:** the noisiest loading tasks.

**Pros:** placing the loading area as far from homes as practical, and avoiding the first and last hours of the day, eases the impact.

**Cons:** compound space is often tight, especially on a seafront.

### Put a proper acoustic barrier around the loading area

**Commonly used for:** fixed or semi-fixed activities like loading bays, crushers and generators.

**Pros:** reduces noise for everyone behind it, all day.

**Cons:** only works if specified properly. A low fence-line barrier won't help much if you look down on the work from upper floors.

## Is an acoustic barrier enough?

On its own, often not. Combined with quieter handling, it can make a real difference. What matters:

- **Height:** it must block the line of sight from your windows to the loading point. Upper floors may need a taller wall. Our guide on [how high an acoustic wall needs to be](/articles/how-high-acoustic-wall-generator-plant-noise) explains how to check.
- **No gaps:** sound leaks through joints and under the base.
- **Close to the source:** the nearer the barrier is to the activity, the better it works.
- **Absorptive face:** **sound absorption (rated as αw) describes how much sound a surface soaks up rather than reflects.** An absorptive face stops noise bouncing back towards other homes.

**Rw is a laboratory rating of how much sound a panel blocks; higher is better.** Lab Rw is not the same as the reduction you'll get on site, because sound diffracts over the top and around the ends of a barrier. For upper-floor flats, see our article on [whether a 4 m wall protects upper floors](/articles/demolition-noise-barrier-4m-acoustic-wall-upper-floor-flats).

## Before you act: checklist

- Have you logged the date, time and description of each bang, and how it affected you?
- Do you know the site's agreed working hours?
- Have you asked the contractor for monitoring results, including LAmax, for the times you logged?
- Have you asked what's being done about impact noise: drop heights, lined truck bodies, location of loading?
- If you're worried about damage, have you asked for vibration data and a survey?
- No progress? Write to Environmental Health with your log, and copy in your ward councillor.
