Barrier spec sheets often boast about "high absorption", "αw 0.70", "NRC 1.0" or even "100% absorption". It sounds impressive, but what does it actually mean for the noise reaching your home? And how is it measured?

This guide explains sound absorption in plain English and why it matters on construction sites.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- What sound absorption is, and how it differs from sound insulation
- How absorption is measured in a laboratory
- What αw, the absorption classes A to E, and NRC mean
- Why "100% absorption" claims need care, with a real lab example
- Why some "acoustic" barriers are, in absorption terms, as reflective as hoarding
- Why an absorbent barrier face matters on site

## What is sound absorption?

**Sound absorption is the amount of sound energy a surface soaks up instead of reflecting back.** Soft, porous materials such as acoustic foam, mineral wool and fibre fleece absorb well, because sound waves lose energy as they move through the tiny air pockets. Hard surfaces such as concrete, steel sheet and glass reflect most sound.

It's given as a **sound absorption coefficient (α)** from 0 to 1:

- **0** means all the sound is reflected
- **1** means all the sound is absorbed
- **0.7** means about 70% is absorbed and 30% reflected

## Sound absorption vs sound insulation: what's the difference?

These are two different jobs, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes with acoustic barriers.

| | Sound absorption | Sound insulation |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Soaks up sound hitting the surface, so less bounces back | Stops sound passing through to the other side |
| Rated by | αw (0 to 1) or NRC | Rw (in dB) |
| Standard | ISO 354 test, ISO 11654 rating | ISO 10140-2 test, ISO 717-1 rating |
| Good materials | Soft, porous: foam, fleece, mineral wool | Heavy, airtight: dense vinyl, mass-loaded layers |

A sheet of acoustic foam on its own absorbs well but lets a lot of sound straight through. A sheet of dense vinyl blocks sound well but reflects it. **A good temporary acoustic barrier combines the two:** a heavy, airtight layer to block sound (good Rw) and a soft, porous face to absorb it (good αw). See [What is the Rw dB rating?](/articles/what-is-rw-rating-acoustic-barrier)

## How is sound absorption measured?

The standard laboratory test is **ISO 354, measurement of sound absorption in a reverberation room.**

1. A reverberation room is a large room with hard, reflective walls, so sound echoes for a long time.
2. Testers make a loud sound, stop it and measure how long the echo takes to die away (the **reverberation time**).
3. They then lay around 10 to 12 square metres of the material in the room and repeat the test.
4. The more absorbent the material, the faster the echo dies away.
5. From the change in reverberation time, they calculate the absorption coefficient at each frequency band, typically from 100 Hz to 5000 Hz.

The result is a set of absorption coefficients, one for each frequency. Many materials absorb high-pitched sound better than low, rumbling sound, but not all: as the example below shows, some barriers are tuned to absorb lower frequencies.

**Can absorption be more than 1?** In a lab report, yes. ISO 354 results sometimes come out above 1.0 because sound also bends round the exposed edges of the sample, making it act larger than it is. It doesn't mean the material absorbs more than 100% of the sound. When the results are turned into a single rating, ISO 11654 caps each value at 1.00.

## What is αw and what do the absorption classes mean?

To make the results easier to compare, **ISO 11654** turns them into one number:

1. The frequency results are grouped into octave bands from 250 Hz to 4000 Hz, giving the **practical absorption coefficient (αp)**.
2. These are compared with a standard reference curve, which is shifted to fit.
3. The value of the shifted curve at 500 Hz is the **weighted sound absorption coefficient, αw.**

ISO 11654 also gives each αw value a class:

| Absorption class | αw |
|---|---|
| A | 0.90 to 1.00 |
| B | 0.80 to 0.85 |
| C | 0.60 to 0.75 |
| D | 0.30 to 0.55 |
| E | 0.15 to 0.25 |
| Not classified | 0.00 to 0.10 |

When you hear people talk about a barrier's "class", this is usually what they mean. There's no equivalent lettered class for Rw.

**What "not classified" means in practice.** A material at αw 0.10 or below absorbs so little that, acoustically, it is a reflective surface, in the same territory as solid timber site hoarding. It can still be a good sound *blocker* if it has a decent Rw, but it won't stop noise bouncing around the site.

**Shape indicators (L), (M) and (H).** Sometimes αw is followed by a letter in brackets. It means that at low (L, 250 Hz), middle (M, 500 to 1000 Hz) or high (H, 2000 to 4000 Hz) frequencies, the material absorbs a lot more than the single number suggests. ISO 11654 recommends reading αw together with the full frequency curve whenever a shape indicator appears.

## What is NRC?

**NRC (Noise Reduction Coefficient) is the American absorption rating, measured to ASTM C423.** It's the average of the absorption coefficients at 250, 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz, rounded to the nearest 0.05. It does a similar job to αw, but the two are calculated differently, so they don't always match. Some suppliers quote both: Hushtec's Performance barrier, for example, is quoted at NRC 1.0 and αw 1.0, Class A. If your project specification asks for αw to ISO 11654, make sure that's the figure you're given.

## What does "100% absorption" mean? A real example

When a spec sheet says a barrier achieves "up to 100% absorption", it usually means the **best frequency range** in the test, not the overall rating.

Echo Barrier's H9 spec sheet says its core can absorb "100% of noise at certain frequencies". Its lab report (University of Salford, UKAS-accredited, January 2019, ISO 354 and ISO 11654) shows exactly what that means:

| Octave band | Practical absorption (αp) |
|---|---|
| 125 Hz | 0.20 |
| 250 Hz | 0.80 |
| 500 Hz | 1.00 |
| 1000 Hz | 0.40 |
| 2000 Hz | 0.15 |
| 4000 Hz | 0.15 |

The H9 is a **very strong absorber around 250 to 500 Hz** (in the detailed results it peaks at 1.19 at 400 Hz), which is useful because a lot of engine and plant noise sits there. Above 1000 Hz, its absorption drops to around 0.15. Rated to ISO 11654, the result is **αw 0.25 (L)(M), Class E**, with the (L)(M) flagging that strong low and mid-frequency performance.

So the "100%" claim and the Class E rating are both true. They just answer different questions. That's why it's worth asking for αw *and* the full frequency curve, and thinking about which frequencies matter on your site.

## How do real temporary barriers compare on absorption?

| Barrier | αw (ISO 11654) | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Hushtec Performance / Premium | 1.0 | A |
| RVT Group SOUNDEX Curtain Plus (enclosure and scaffold curtain) | 0.70 | C |
| Echo Barrier H9 | 0.25 (L)(M) | E |
| RVT Group SOUNDEX Quilt (fence-mounted) | 0.20 | E |
| Siderise NRB Superflex | 0.10 | Not classified (reflective) |

*From manufacturer documents and test reports, November 2026.*

The Siderise NRB Superflex is worth singling out. Its own data sheet (v6.00, June 2024) gives it a respectable Rw 22 dB for blocking sound, but an absorption rating of just αw 0.10. That puts it below Class E, so for absorption purposes it behaves like a reflective surface, much like hoarding. Note too that RVT's Quilt and Curtain Plus are separate products with very different figures, so always check which exact product a number belongs to.

For permanent roadside noise barriers there's a separate test, **BS EN 1793-1**, which rates absorption as a single figure in dB (DLα) rather than a 0 to 1 coefficient. You may see it on some barrier data sheets.

## Why does sound absorption matter on a construction site?

Because a reflective barrier can make some things worse:

- **Noise bounces back across the site** and then out over the top of the barrier or towards homes on the other side.
- **Sound builds up between the barrier and the machine**, especially in tight sites or narrow streets with buildings close by.
- **Workers on site** are exposed to more noise.

An absorbent face soaks up some of that energy instead. That's why **the soft, padded side of a barrier should always face the works.** If it's been fitted the wrong way round, ask for it to be turned.

But absorption alone won't protect your home. A barrier also needs a decent Rw, enough height to hide the machine from your window, and no gaps. ## Quick checklist for residents

- Is the padded, absorbent face pointing towards the works?
- Is the barrier tall enough to hide the machine from your windows?
- Are there gaps at joints or under the base?
- Has the contractor told you the barrier's Rw and αw? If the αw is 0.10 or below, it's a reflective barrier.
