Acoustic barriers are the most common mitigation measure on noisy construction sites, and also the most commonly misrepresented. A barrier that is too short, poorly sealed or in the wrong place can reduce noise by almost nothing while appearing on a mitigation plan.

## How a barrier reduces noise

A barrier does not absorb the site's noise. It interrupts the path between the source and the listener, forcing sound to travel over the top edge. That detour is longer than the direct path, and the difference — the **path length difference** — is what produces the reduction.

The practical consequence: **a barrier only works if it breaks the line of sight**. If a resident can see the piling rig over the top of the panels, the barrier is contributing very little.

## What performance is realistic

For a correctly specified barrier close to the source:

| Situation | Realistic reduction |
| --- | --- |
| Line of sight fully broken, barrier 3–4 m high near the plant | 10–15 dB |
| Taller barrier or plant in a compound with returns | 15–20 dB |
| Barrier present but line of sight not broken | 0–5 dB |
| Low-frequency impact noise from piling | 3–8 dB less than the figures above |

## Where barriers fall short

- **Low frequency.** Impact piling produces strong energy at 20–100 Hz, which diffracts over and around barriers far more readily than mid frequencies.
- **Vibration.** Barriers reduce airborne noise only. Ground vibration passes underneath entirely and is dealt with by choosing a different piling method or interrupting the vibration path.
- **Gaps.** A 50 mm gap in a run of panels can dominate the performance of the whole barrier. Sealing at ground level and between panels matters as much as the panel specification.
- **Height and distance.** A barrier half a metre too short at close range can lose most of its benefit.

## What to ask a site to provide

If you are a contractor drawing up a Section 61 application, or a resident reviewing a mitigation plan, these are the questions that separate a real barrier from a line on a drawing:

1. What is the barrier height, and does it break the line of sight from the nearest dwellings?
2. What surface mass do the panels have, and are they sealed at ground level and at joints?
3. How close is the barrier to the plant, and does it return around the compound?
4. What reduction has been predicted, and by whom?
