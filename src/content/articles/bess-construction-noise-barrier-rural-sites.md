Battery energy storage (BESS) sites are often built where there is space and a grid connection: farmland, the edge of a village, a lane that is normally silent after dark. That is exactly where construction noise is hardest to ignore. In a quiet area, BS 5228-1 usually puts nearby homes in its strictest category, with an example daytime threshold of 65 dB LAeq.

If you live nearby, a site that suddenly brings generators, lorries and reversing alarms to a quiet lane can feel like your home has changed overnight. If you run the site, you may be getting complaints even though the work would barely register in a town.

This guide explains why BESS construction noise behaves differently on rural sites and what good screening looks like.

**In this article you'll learn:**

- Why low background noise makes construction noise stand out
- What BS 5228-1 says about thresholds in quiet areas
- How high a BESS noise barrier needs to be, with a simple rule of thumb
- How to judge whether a barrier is any good (Rw, αw, gaps and position)
- What to ask the contractor or the council for

## Why is BESS construction noise such a problem on rural sites?

It depends mainly on three things: how quiet the area is to begin with, which activities are happening, and how close they are to homes.

**The background is low.** In a town, traffic covers a lot of site noise. In the countryside there is little to mask it, especially in the evening and at weekends. You hear the site because there is nothing else to hear.

**The work is spread out.** A BESS build can include access tracks, groundworks, foundations for the battery units, cable trenching, crane lifts and a steady flow of deliveries. If foundations are piled, see our [pile driver noise hub](/pile-driver-noise). Before the grid connection is live, generators may power the welfare cabins and tools.

**Noise carries.** Open fields offer little in the way of buildings or trees to break up sound, and low-pitched engine noise travels further than higher-pitched sound.

### Is construction noise the same as the hum from a finished BESS site?

No, and it helps to keep them apart. Once running, a BESS site produces a steady hum. Energy-Storage.news reports that battery units mainly emit noise from their cooling systems, with inverters and transformers adding to it. Inverters and transformers can also be tonal, a buzz at twice the grid frequency, which is 100 Hz on the UK's 50 Hz grid. That operational noise is controlled through planning conditions and the permanent site design. This article is about the temporary construction phase.

## What does BS 5228-1 say about quiet areas?

**BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014 is the UK code of practice for predicting and controlling construction noise.** Its Annex E includes the "ABC method" for judging whether construction noise at homes is likely to have a significant effect.

The assessor measures the existing ambient noise, rounds it to the nearest 5 dB and compares it with the Category A values. If the ambient level is below them, as it usually is in quiet countryside, the home falls into Category A, the lowest thresholds.

| Period | Category A | Category B | Category C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Night-time (23:00–07:00) | 45 dB | 50 dB | 55 dB |
| Evenings and weekends | 55 dB | 60 dB | 65 dB |
| Daytime (07:00–19:00) and Saturdays (07:00–13:00) | 65 dB | 70 dB | 75 dB |

*Example thresholds of potential significant effect at dwellings, LAeq,T, from Table E.1 of BS 5228-1. Evenings and weekends means 19:00–23:00 weekdays, 13:00–23:00 Saturdays and 07:00–23:00 Sundays.*

**LAeq is the average sound level over a period, A-weighted (dB(A)) to match how the human ear hears.** If site noise goes above the category value, a potential significant effect is indicated, and the assessor then looks at how many homes are affected and how long the work lasts.

Even a site that stays under these thresholds can be clearly audible in a quiet lane. That is why screening the noisiest work matters more here than on a busy urban site.

## How high does a BESS noise barrier need to be?

**Hushtec's working rule of thumb is that an acoustic screen should be about 1.5 m higher than the top of the noise source.** So a 2.5 m generator needs a 4 m wall.

| Top of noise source | Screen height to aim for |
|---|---|
| 1.0 m (pump, small compressor) | 2.5 m |
| 2.5 m (large site generator) | 4 m |
| 3.5 m (excavator cab and engine) | 5 m |
| 4.5 m | 6 m |

Treat this as a first estimate, not a substitute for a noise assessment. It assumes listeners at ground level. Windows that overlook the source, or homes on higher ground, need a taller wall or one placed closer to the source. The reasoning is covered in [How high does an acoustic wall need to be to screen generator and plant noise?](/articles/how-high-acoustic-wall-generator-plant-noise)

Why does height matter so much? BS 5228-1 gives a working approximation: a barrier reduces noise by about 5 dB when the top of the plant is just visible over it, and about 10 dB when the plant is completely hidden. A 10 dB drop sounds roughly half as loud.

## What makes a good acoustic barrier?

Whether you are looking over the hedge or choosing a product, check these points:

- **Height above the line of sight.** You should not see the top of the plant from the nearest windows.
- **No gaps.** Joints overlap or are sealed, and the base meets the ground. Uneven rural ground makes this harder.
- **Long enough.** The screen runs well past both ends of the work, or wraps round it.
- **Close to the source.** A barrier works best close to the plant or close to the homes, not halfway between.
- **Absorptive face towards the works.** A hard face reflects noise elsewhere.
- **Rated for wind.** Open fields are exposed, and a tall solid screen catches a lot of wind.
- **A published Rw and αw**, with the test standards named.

### What does Rw mean?

**Rw (weighted sound reduction index) is a single-number lab rating of how much sound a material stops passing through it, calculated to ISO 717-1 from measurements made to ISO 10140.** A panel rated Rw 26 dB cuts sound passing straight through it by roughly 26 dB in the lab.

On site you will not hear the full figure. Sound bends over the top and round the ends, so the real reduction (the **insertion loss**, the difference with and without the barrier) depends mostly on height, length and position.

### What does αw mean?

**αw (weighted sound absorption coefficient) is a rating from 0 to 1, set under ISO 11654, of how much sound a surface soaks up rather than reflects.** An αw of 1.0 is Class A, the highest. An αw of 0.10 or below is not classified under ISO 11654 and is effectively reflective, like plywood hoarding.

## What are the options for BESS construction noise mitigation?

Start at the source: locate generators and compounds away from homes, keep deliveries to agreed hours and routes, use broadband reversing alarms, and switch plant off when idle. Then screen what is left.

| Option | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Fence-mounted acoustic panels (about 2 m) | Low plant, hand tools, the site boundary | Too low for generators and excavators |
| Close-in screen or enclosure around plant | Generators, pumps, fixed plant | Plant still needs cooling air; agree with the supplier |
| Earth bunds from site soil | Long-term screening on large sites | Space, time and planning; not quick to move |
| Scaffold-mounted barrier | Tall screens where scaffold is available | Wind design on exposed sites |
| Lined shipping containers | Heavy, long-term screens | Cranage, footprint, reflective unless lined |
| Freestanding acoustic wall, 4 m to 8 m | Generator compounds, work near the nearest homes | Needs suitable ground and access; costs more than fence panels |

### Who is a tall wall NOT right for?

- **Short, mobile work** such as trenching that moves along the site each day. Hours and plant choice will usually do more.
- **Homes that look down on the site from higher ground.** A wall will not hide the work; an enclosure at source is better.
- **The finished BESS site's hum.** That needs a permanent design, not a temporary construction screen.

## What can you ask the contractor or the council for?

Under Section 60 of the Control of Pollution Act 1974, the council can serve a notice setting the plant that may be used, the hours of work and the noise levels allowed. Contractors can apply in advance for consent under Section 61.

2. What site noise is predicted at my home, and for how long?
3. Where will generators and compounds go, and how will they be screened?
4. What are the working and delivery hours, including weekends?
5. Who do I contact if the noise goes above what was agreed?

## Before you choose or complain: checklist

- Find out the ambient level and the BS 5228-1 category for the nearest homes.
- Take the height of the top of each noise source and add about 1.5 m as a first estimate.
- Check the view from the highest window and from any higher ground.
- Look for gaps at joints and at the base, and for screens that stop short.
- Ask for the Rw and αw, with the test standards named, and the wind rating.
- Keep a dated record of noise, especially early mornings, evenings and weekends.
