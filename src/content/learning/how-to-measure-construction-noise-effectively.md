Documenting noise levels accurately can make your complaint far more credible. Here's how to do it properly.

## What Unit is Noise Measured In?

Noise is measured in **decibels (dB)**. The scale is logarithmic — meaning 80 dB is not twice as loud as 40 dB, it's many times louder.

## UK Noise Thresholds for Construction

| Time | Typical guideline |
|------|---------------|
| Daytime (07:00–19:00 weekdays) | Around 65–75 dB LAeq at the nearest facade, depending on the existing background level |
| Night-time (23:00–07:00) | Much lower — night work normally needs prior agreement with the council |

These figures come from the assessment method in BS 5228-1:2009+A1:2014, the British Standard code of practice for noise and vibration control on construction and open sites. Your council may set specific limits for a site through a Section 60 notice or Section 61 consent.

## Using Decibel X on Your Phone

The **Decibel X** app (available on iOS and Android) is widely regarded as one of the most accurate smartphone sound level meters. To get reliable readings:

- Hold your phone at arm's length, pointing towards the noise source
- Take readings over at least 30 seconds to capture a meaningful average
- Note the **LAeq** value — this is the equivalent continuous sound level and is what councils and courts typically refer to
- Screenshot or export the data with a timestamp

## Tips for Reliable Evidence

- Record from multiple positions (inside your home, at your front door, at the garden boundary)
- Take readings on different days and at different times
- Note the weather — wind can affect readings
- Keep a written log alongside your measurements

## What If Levels Are Below the Threshold?

Even if measured levels are below these guideline figures, you may still have grounds to complain if the noise is a **statutory nuisance** under the Environmental Protection Act 1990 — this is a qualitative assessment, not purely numerical.
