**Piling** is one of the most significant sources of construction noise in urban areas. But the term covers a wide variety of techniques — and their noise profiles vary enormously. Understanding which type of piling is being used near you helps you assess whether the noise levels are reasonable, and strengthens any complaint you make.

## Why Piling Type Matters for Noise Complaints

Unmitigated piling noise can range from around **70 dB** for quiet rotary bored methods up to **105 dB or more** at source for driven piling. That 35 dB difference is enormous — on the logarithmic decibel scale, it represents a noise source roughly 3,000 times more acoustically powerful. Knowing what type of piling is being used is therefore the first step in assessing whether the noise is reasonable or excessive.

## Category 1: Rotary / Bored Piling — The Quieter Methods

These methods drill or bore into the ground rather than forcing piles in by impact. They are significantly quieter than driven methods and are increasingly preferred on noise-sensitive sites.

### CFA — Continuous Flight Auger
**The most common piling method in the UK.**

A large continuous helical auger is rotated and drilled into the ground in a single uninterrupted operation. As the auger is withdrawn, concrete is pumped at high pressure through the hollow stem, filling the bore from the bottom up. The reinforcement cage is then pushed into the wet concrete.

- **Noise profile:** Relatively low. The dominant sound is the hydraulic rig motor and the rotation of the auger — a continuous, low-frequency drone rather than impact noise
- **Typical source SPL:** 75–85 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Residential foundations, commercial buildings, retaining walls

### Rotary Bored Piling
A rotating bucket or auger cuts into the ground inside a steel casing (temporary or permanent). The casing prevents bore collapse in unstable ground. Slower than CFA but suitable for difficult or variable ground conditions.

- **Noise profile:** Similar to CFA — continuous mechanical noise from the rig, no impact
- **Typical source SPL:** 75–85 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Large-diameter piles, bridge foundations, complex ground conditions

### Mini Piling
A small-diameter version of rotary bored piling, typically 150–300 mm diameter. Used where access is restricted — inside existing buildings, in gardens, or where headroom is limited.

- **Noise profile:** Low — smaller rigs generate less noise than full-size piling plant
- **Typical source SPL:** 70–80 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Underpinning, extensions, restricted access sites

## Category 2: Driven Piling — The High-Noise Category

These methods force pre-formed piles into the ground using impact or vibratory energy. They are the primary source of serious piling noise complaints.

### Precast Concrete Pile (Driven)
A pre-formed reinforced concrete pile is positioned vertically and driven into the ground blow by blow using a drop hammer or hydraulic hammer. Each blow sends a sharp, high-energy impulse through the pile and into the surrounding ground.

- **Noise profile:** High-impact, impulsive noise with strong low-frequency content. Each hammer blow produces a distinct loud thud
- **Typical source SPL:** **95–105 dB** at 10 metres (unmitigated)
- **Common uses:** Commercial and infrastructure projects, high-load foundations

### Steel Sheet Piling (Driven)
Interlocking steel sections (Z, U, or box profiles) are driven vertically to form continuous retaining walls, cofferdams, or basement cut-offs.

- **Noise profile:** Very high impact noise, with the additional metallic ringing of steel-on-steel contact. Often perceived as one of the most intrusive forms of construction noise
- **Typical source SPL:** 95–105 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Basement construction, flood defences, temporary works

### Steel H/I Pile (Driven)
Structural steel H-section or I-section piles driven by impact hammer, with a high-frequency steel ring on each blow.

- **Noise profile:** High impact, with metallic ringing component
- **Typical source SPL:** 95–103 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Bridge abutments, jetties, heavy industrial foundations

### Tubular Steel Pile (Driven or Vibrated)
Large diameter hollow steel tubes, either driven by impact hammer or installed using a vibratory hammer. Sometimes filled with concrete after installation.

- **Noise profile:** High when driven; moderate when vibrated
- **Typical source SPL:** 90–105 dB at 10 metres (impact); 80–95 dB (vibratory)
- **Common uses:** Offshore structures, ports, heavy civil engineering

## Category 3: Vibratory / Vibro Methods

### Vibratory Hammer
Instead of impact blows, a vibratory hammer uses rapidly oscillating eccentric weights to generate vertical vibration. This reduces the friction between the pile and the soil, allowing the pile to sink under its own weight plus the weight of the hammer.

- **Noise profile:** A continuous high-frequency drone or hum rather than discrete blows. Still significant but less impulsive
- **Typical source SPL:** 80–95 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Sheet piling installation and extraction, tubular piles in granular soils

### Vibro Compaction / Stone Columns
A ground improvement technique rather than structural piling. A large vibrating probe is inserted into granular soil to compact it, and aggregate (stone) is added to form load-bearing columns.

- **Noise profile:** Similar continuous drone to vibratory hammer, but typically lower
- **Typical source SPL:** 75–90 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Ground improvement under new developments

---

## Category 4: Displacement / Specialist Methods

### Screw Pile / Helical Pile
A steel pile with helical flights (like a giant screw) rotated into the ground using a hydraulic torque motor.

- **Noise profile:** Very low — often cited as the quietest piling method available
- **Typical source SPL:** 70–80 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Noise-sensitive sites, solar farms, temporary structures, low-load applications

### Franki Pile / Driven Cast In-Situ
A steel drive tube is driven into the ground by a drop hammer acting on a plug of dry concrete or aggregate. At depth, more hammer blows ram concrete out of the base to form an enlarged bulb, then the tube is withdrawn as the shaft concrete is placed.

- **Noise profile:** Very high impact noise on every blow, similar to precast driven piling
- **Typical source SPL:** 95–105 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** High-load foundations

### Micropile / Ground Anchor
Small diameter (typically 100–300 mm) drilled and grouted pile or anchor.

- **Noise profile:** Low — similar to mini piling, dominated by drill motor noise
- **Typical source SPL:** 70–82 dB at 10 metres
- **Common uses:** Retaining wall anchors, underpinning, slope stabilisation

## How Much Can Driven Piling Noise Be Reduced?

Driven piling doesn't have to run unscreened. There are three main ways to cut the airborne noise, and they work in different places:

| Option | Where it works | Reduction | When the rig moves |
|--------|----------------|-----------|--------------------|
| Perimeter barrier (e.g. RapidWall, up to 8 m high) | Site boundary | Depends on height and position; panels rated Rw 32 dB, αw 1.0 | Stays at the boundary |
| Freestanding shroud around the active pile | At the pile | 15–21 dB | A few hours to relocate |
| Rig-mounted shroud around the hammer | At the hammer | 7.2 dB at 5 m | Travels with the rig |

Measured results for hammer shrouds (field comparison):

| Distance | No shroud | Half shroud | Full shroud |
|----------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| 5 m | 100.0 dB | 93.2 dB | 92.8 dB |
| 10 m | 94.7 dB | 88.3 dB | 88.2 dB |
| 15 m | 89.9 dB | 86.7 dB | 82.5 dB |

*Full shroud measured at Woodhall Spa, half shroud at Ramsey; no-shroud values are a published baseline. Readings come from separate trials and are indicative. Product data from Hushtec, which supports this site. Results vary with rig, pile, ground and measurement conditions.*

## What This Means for Your Complaint

**Driven precast concrete and driven steel piling generate the highest levels** — often 95–105 dB unmitigated — and are the most likely to exceed BS 5228 thresholds and constitute a statutory nuisance.

If you are being affected by piling noise:
1. Try to identify the piling method being used (ask the site manager, check the planning documents, or observe the equipment)
2. Use the table above to understand whether the noise level you are experiencing is proportionate to the method
3. Check whether the contractor is using any mitigation — a shroud around the hammer (around 7 dB reduction), an enclosure around the active pile (15–21 dB), a perimeter acoustic barrier, or restricted operating hours are all recognised best practicable means under BS 5228
4. If driven piling is being used next to homes without mitigation, this is a strong basis for a formal complaint to your council's Environmental Health team
