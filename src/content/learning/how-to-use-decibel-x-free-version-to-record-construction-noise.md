**Decibel X** is one of the most accurate sound level meter apps available for smartphones, and the free version provides all the key features you need to document construction noise effectively. This guide walks you through everything from setup to evidence export.

## What is Decibel X?

Decibel X (also listed as **dB Meter Pro** or **DecibelX** on some app stores) turns your phone's microphone into a sound level meter. It gives real-time dB readings and displays averaged and peak levels.

**Download links:**
- **iOS (iPhone/iPad):** Search "Decibel X" on the App Store — look for the app by SkyPaw Co. Ltd
- **Android:** Search "Decibel X" on Google Play Store

The free version is fully functional for noise complaint purposes. A paid "Pro" version adds additional logging features, but you do not need it.

## Step 1: Setting Up the App

When you first open Decibel X:

1. **Grant microphone permission** – the app will ask to access your microphone; tap Allow
2. **Choose the correct weighting** – set to **dB(A)** weighting. This is the standard used in UK guidance and is what Environmental Health Officers use
3. **Set the response time** to **Slow** – for construction noise, Slow smooths out brief spikes and gives a more representative reading
4. **Check calibration** – in a quiet room, your reading should show approximately 30–40 dB. If it shows significantly higher in silence, your phone's microphone may be picking up wind noise

## Step 2: Understanding the Display

- **Current dB level** – the large number in the centre, updating in real time
- **MAX** – the highest peak recorded during your current session
- **MIN** – the lowest level recorded
- **AVG** – the average level over the measurement period. This corresponds to the **LAeq** value used in UK assessments and is your most important figure

**The AVG/LAeq value is what matters most for UK noise complaints.** Councils and courts assess construction noise against LAeq levels, not momentary peaks.

## Step 3: Taking a Measurement

### Positioning
- Hold your phone at **arm's length** pointing towards the noise source
- Do **not** cup your hand around the phone — this affects the microphone
- Avoid holding the phone close to your body, which can cause reflections
- If measuring indoors, stand close to (but not directly in front of) an open window

### Duration
- Record for at least **60 seconds** to capture a reliable average
- For intermittent noise (e.g. drilling that starts and stops), try to capture **at least two full cycles** of the loudest activity
- For a more formal complaint, aim for **5–10 minutes** of recording to capture the true sustained level

### Multiple Locations
Take readings from at least **three positions**:
1. Inside your home (living room or bedroom — whichever is most affected)
2. At your front door or doorstep
3. At the boundary of your property closest to the noise source

Record the AVG reading at each position, along with the time and your exact location.

## Step 4: Capturing Evidence

### Taking a Screenshot
After your measurement period:
1. Press the **pause button** to freeze the readings
2. Note down the AVG, MAX, and MIN values displayed
3. Take a **screenshot** of the screen
4. The screenshot will include the readings and your phone's **timestamp** visible in the notification bar

### Using the History / Log Feature
In the free version, you can access a basic session history. After each measurement, note the reading in your own written log alongside the date, time, address, and type of noise. This written log, combined with your screenshots, forms a strong evidence pack.

## Step 5: What the Numbers Mean

Use this rough guide when interpreting daytime AVG readings taken outside your home:

| Average Reading (LAeq) | Interpretation |
|------------------------|----------------|
| Below 60 dB | Elevated background noise — unlikely to support a complaint on its own |
| 60–65 dB | Noticeable — approaching the lowest BS 5228 daytime threshold |
| 65–75 dB | Within the range where BS 5228 treats construction noise as potentially significant, depending on the area's usual background |
| Above 75 dB | Above the highest BS 5228 daytime threshold — strong basis for a formal complaint |
| Above 85 dB | Potential hearing damage risk for prolonged exposure |

**Note:** At night, evenings and weekends the thresholds are far lower, so even moderate readings during those hours can support a complaint.

## Step 6: Wind and Environmental Interference

Wind is the most common cause of inaccurate smartphone readings. If it is windy:
- Shield the phone microphone with your body (without covering it)
- Note the weather conditions in your written log
- Consider taking readings on a calmer day for comparison

Other interference to note: traffic passing close by, aircraft (pause and restart), and rain.

## Step 7: Exporting and Sharing Your Evidence

1. Create a dedicated folder on your phone called "Noise Evidence"
2. Save all screenshots there, keeping notes with the date and location
3. When writing to your council's Environmental Health team, attach screenshots to your email
4. Reference the readings explicitly: e.g. *"On 15 March at 09:30, I recorded an average LAeq of 78 dB at the boundary of my property at [address]."*

## Tips for Court-Ready Evidence

- **Record consistently** — same positions, same app settings, multiple dates
- **Accompany readings with a written log** — date, time, weather, description of the noise, and your reading
- **Take video on a second device** simultaneously if possible, so the noise and the app screen are captured together
- **Note the phone model** — some models are more accurate than others
- **Keep raw screenshots unedited** — do not crop or alter the images

## Limitations of Smartphone Measurements

- Smartphone microphones are not **Class 1 or Class 2 sound level meters** as defined by IEC 61672, so councils and courts may treat readings as indicative rather than conclusive
- For the strongest evidence, consider an independent acoustic consultant if your complaint is serious
- That said, consistent smartphone readings from multiple sessions significantly strengthen a complaint and are widely accepted as supporting evidence by Environmental Health Officers
