Under the **Environmental Protection Act 1990 (EPA)**, a council has a duty to take action when something constitutes a **statutory nuisance**. But what exactly does that mean?

## The Legal Definition

Section 79 of the EPA 1990 defines a statutory nuisance as something that:
- **Unreasonably and substantially interferes** with the use and enjoyment of land or premises, or
- **Is prejudicial to health**

The key word is *unreasonably*. Construction during permitted hours, even if disruptive, may not meet the threshold — it has to be unreasonable in the circumstances.

## How Councils Assess It

An Environmental Health Officer (EHO) will typically:
1. Visit the site or your property to witness the noise
2. Take decibel measurements
3. Consider the time of day, frequency, and duration
4. Weigh this against what is considered "normal" for the area

## What Happens if a Statutory Nuisance Is Found?

Under **Section 80 EPA 1990**, the council *must* serve an **Abatement Notice** on the person responsible. This legally requires them to:
- Stop the nuisance, or
- Restrict it to certain hours or levels, or
- Carry out works to prevent its recurrence

Failure to comply with an Abatement Notice is a criminal offence, with fines of up to **£20,000** for industrial/commercial premises.

## What If the Council Won't Act?

If you believe a statutory nuisance exists but the council refuses to investigate or act, you can bring proceedings yourself in a **magistrates' court** under **Section 82 EPA 1990**. For noise, you must first give the person responsible at least **three days'** written notice of your intention (for other kinds of nuisance the notice period is 21 days).

This is a powerful but underused right. Citizens Advice or a solicitor can help you navigate the process.
